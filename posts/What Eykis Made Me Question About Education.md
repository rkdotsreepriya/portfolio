# What Eykis Made Me Question About Education

There are some things we do so often that we stop noticing how strange they are.

We sit in particular places. We follow particular routines. We accept certain rules. We measure success in particular ways. And because everyone around us does the same thing, we call it normal.

But what happens when someone from outside our world looks at all of this for the first time?

That was one of the uncomfortable thoughts I was left with while reading Gifts from Eykis by Wayne Dyer.

The book did not make me think about education because it is a book about education. It made me think about education because it made ordinary things look unfamiliar.

And when you work with children, that can be a slightly dangerous way of looking at the world.

Because children are very good at noticing what adults have stopped noticing.

![Eykis](/assets/eyes.jpg)

## Seeing Earth Through Eykis

The premise of Gifts from Eykis is simple. The narrator encounters Eykis, a woman from another planet. Her world looks remarkably similar to ours, but the way people think and live is very different.

Eykis becomes an outsider looking at Earth.

And that is what makes the book interesting.

She doesn't simply tell us that something is wrong. She asks, in effect, Why do you people do this?

Things that seem completely ordinary to us suddenly become strange when viewed from the outside.

The book explores worry, guilt, relationships, work, society, education and many other parts of human life. Its central device is not complicated: change the observer, and suddenly the familiar becomes questionable.

I found myself doing the same thing while reading.

Except my question kept becoming:

### What would Eykis think of our schools?

And more importantly:

### What would she think we are doing to children?

### When Education Becomes Conditioning

I have spent a significant part of my life around preschool, children, educators, parents, curriculum and education systems.

Running Beyond Born Early Education Centre has made me look at learning differently over the years.

The more time I spend around young children, the more convinced I become that children don't need to be taught curiosity.

They already have it.

They ask why constantly.

They touch things.

They experiment.

They repeat an action just to see what happens.

They create explanations that make perfect sense to them.

They ask questions whose answers adults sometimes don't even know.

They learn through play without needing someone to tell them that they are "learning."

And then something slowly changes.

Children begin to worry about being right.

They start looking at the adult before answering.

They become conscious of whether their drawing is "correct."

They ask whether their answer is what the teacher wants.

They sometimes stop exploring because they don't want to make a mistake.

That transition has always bothered me.

At what point does learning become performance?

At what point does a child move from asking, "What happens if I try this?" to asking, "What answer do you want?"

Those are very different ways of learning.

And perhaps we don't notice the difference because the second one often produces better results in a conventional classroom.

###    What Are We Calling "Normal"?

One of the ideas I kept returning to while reading Eykis was the question of what we consider normal.

We don't have to decide that everything traditional is wrong.

Sitting quietly can be useful.

Memorising something can be useful.

Examinations can have a purpose.

Grades can provide information.

Structure matters.

Discipline matters.

The question is not whether these things have value.

The question is whether we have stopped asking **why** we are doing them.
![Book note](assets/book1.jpg)

### Why should every child sit and learn in exactly the same way?

### Why do we often treat the child who finishes first as more capable?

Why is a mistake sometimes treated as evidence that a child has failed rather than evidence that the child is trying something?

Why do we compare children who have completely different interests, personalities, strengths and ways of understanding?

Why do we give one answer more value than a thoughtful question?

And perhaps the bigger question:

What exactly are we measuring when we give a child a mark?

A mark can tell us something.

But it cannot tell us everything.

It cannot measure curiosity very well.

It cannot easily measure imagination.

It cannot tell us whether a child knows how to ask a useful question.

It cannot tell us whether the child can approach an unfamiliar problem without immediately looking for someone to provide the answer.

And it certainly cannot tell us whether we have raised someone who is comfortable thinking differently.

# "Keep It Simple"

One of the lines associated with Eykis is: "Keep it simple."

It sounds almost too obvious.

But I found myself thinking about it in education.

We sometimes make learning complicated because we have complicated the system around learning.

We create elaborate terminology.

We create layers of assessment.

We create increasingly detailed documentation.

We sometimes spend so much time proving that learning is happening that we forget to observe the child actually learning.

A three-year-old discovering why something floats does not need a complicated explanation of the learning outcome before the discovery becomes meaningful.

The child needs space to wonder.

To test.

To fail.

To try again.

To ask another question.

There is a difference between simplifying education and making education simplistic.

I don't think children need less learning.

I think they need fewer unnecessary barriers between themselves and the experience of learning.

What Happens to Imagination When There Is Always a Right Answer?

This is something I think about often.

Young children can be wonderfully imaginative because they haven't yet learned all the boundaries adults have created.

A box can become a house.

A stick can become a telescope.

A piece of paper can become almost anything.

There isn't necessarily one correct way to use it.

But as children move through education, we increasingly tell them what the answer should look like.

Colour inside the lines.

Write this way.

Solve it this way.

Give this answer.

Remember this.

Repeat this.

Don't make mistakes.

Follow the instructions.

Again, none of these instructions is automatically wrong.

But taken together, they can create something we should be careful about.

A child may slowly learn that approval is safer than exploration.

That is when education can become conditioning.

Not because teachers intend to condition children.

Not because parents want to suppress curiosity.

But because systems tend to reward behaviours that make the system easier to manage.

The quiet child is easier to manage.

The child who follows instructions is easier to assess.

The child who gives the expected answer is easier to reward.

The child who asks difficult questions can be harder.

And yet, that difficult question may be the most valuable thing happening in the room.

What I Want Education to Protect

My work in early childhood education has increasingly made me think that education has another responsibility.

It should protect certain things that children already possess.

Curiosity.

Imagination.

The confidence to make mistakes.

The ability to ask questions.

The willingness to experiment.

The freedom to say, "I don't know."

The confidence to say, "I think differently."

The joy of discovering something without immediately asking whether it will come in an examination.

These things sound simple.

But perhaps that is the problem.

We sometimes underestimate simple things because they don't fit neatly into a measurable outcome.

At Beyond Born, I find myself thinking about these questions while looking at curriculum, activities, learning environments and the role of educators.

Not because I believe we have figured it out.

Quite the opposite.

The more I work in education, the more questions I have.

And I think that is probably a good thing.

What Am I Teaching Them to Accept?

The book also made me question myself.

It is easy to look at education systems and point out what needs to change.

It is much harder to look at our own behaviour.

So I asked myself:

Am I sometimes trying to teach children to fit into the world rather than helping them understand and question the world?

That is not an easy question.

As educators, we have responsibilities. We need routines. We need boundaries. We need to help children function with others. We need to prepare them for the world they are going to enter.

But there is a fine line between preparing a child for the world and preparing a child to accept the world exactly as it is.

I don't think education should teach children to reject every rule.

That would be just another form of conditioning.

I think it should teach them something more difficult:

Understand the rule. Understand why it exists. Know when it matters. And have the courage to question it when necessary.

That is a very different kind of education.

Perhaps the Question Is the Real Learning

There is a line from the book that stayed with me: "You are perfect."

I don't read that as saying children don't need guidance or growth.

I read it differently.

Perhaps children do not need to begin their education believing that something is wrong with them and that school exists to fix them.

Perhaps education should begin with the assumption that there is already something valuable there.

Our job is not simply to fill the empty spaces.

It is to help that person discover what they can do, what they can think, what they care about, and what they might change.

Reading Gifts from Eykis did not give me a new educational philosophy.

It gave me a slightly different pair of eyes.

And perhaps that is more useful.

Because sometimes the most important thing we can do in education is step outside our own system for a moment and look back at it.

To ask why children sit the way they sit.

Why they learn the way they learn.

Why we measure what we measure.

Why mistakes make us uncomfortable.

Why questions sometimes feel like interruptions.

And why a child who wants to know why is sometimes treated as a child who doesn't know how to listen.

Perhaps the real work of education is not only deciding what children should learn.

It is deciding what parts of their natural way of learning we must be careful not to take away.

And perhaps, before asking whether our children are learning enough, we should ask something more uncomfortable:

What are they learning to accept without questioning?

That is the question Eykis left me with.

And I don't think I have finished answering it.
