# When a Teacher Leaves, What Happens to the Trust?

A while back, a few educators at my preschool resigned around the same time. Nothing dramatic. No scandal. People just moved on, the way people do.

As the founder, my first thought was: *this is going to be hard on the children.* I started planning for it like a small operation, how to introduce the new teacher, how to keep the classroom feeling familiar, how long it would take before the kids stopped looking around for someone who wasn't there anymore.

Turns out, I didn't need most of that plan.

The children who'd been closest to their old teacher, the ones who ran to her every morning, who cried if she left the room for five minutes,  settled in with the new educator faster than I expected. Within days, sometimes hours, they were playing with her, holding her hand, chatting away. It's not that they didn't miss their old teacher. It's that they didn't need to sort that feeling out before moving on to the next one.

The parents were a different story.

I got calls. Messages. A few visits. Some people asked straight out. Others were more careful about it, so as not to sound like they were blaming me. A few just went quiet in a way I had to read into. None of it was hostile. It felt more like fear.

And that's what stuck with me: *why is this so much harder for the adults than it is for the three-year-old who actually has to walk into that classroom every day?*

## Why parents find it so hard

I want to be careful here, because it would be easy to write this off as parents being overprotective. That's not what I saw. What I saw was love, running into uncertainty.

When you hand your child over to a preschool, you're not really handing them to a building or a curriculum. You're handing them to a person. That person knows your daughter needs a nap by 11. That your son won't eat if the rice touches the dal. That she gets overwhelmed by loud group songs and needs a minute alone. All of that lives inside a relationship — and when the relationship changes, parents lose the one thing they'd built their confidence on.

So the questions come, and they're fair questions. Will the new teacher get my child the way the old one did? Will she still get the same attention, or is she just another face in someone's classroom now? Does he know the small things, the blanket at naptime, the gentle voice she needs when she's overwhelmed? Will the routine change? Can I trust this person with my child, this soon?

And under all of it, one bigger question: *why did she really leave, is something wrong with this school?*

I've come to think that for a lot of parents, the teacher isn't just a teacher. She's the proof that the school is safe. If she's warm and steady, the whole place feels solid. If she leaves suddenly, that feeling of solidness wobbles too,  even if nothing else has actually changed. Parents had put their trust in a person. That trust doesn't just carry over to the next person. It has to be earned again, from scratch.

## Why children seem to adapt faster

The fact that children settle in quickly doesn't mean they don't care. It just means they experience relationships differently than we do.

A three- or four-year-old isn't sitting there tallying up how many teachers she's had this year, or wondering what it means for the future. She's mostly responding to what's right in front of her and if what's in front of her is warm and paying attention, that's often enough to start trusting again.

There's a concept researchers at Harvard's Center on the Developing Child call "serve and return", the back-and-forth between a child reaching out (a smile, a question, a cry) and an adult responding to it. It's this responsiveness, more than any one specific person, that seems to build a child's sense of safety over time. What a young child needs most is someone showing up for them consistently, not necessarily the exact same someone forever.

Research also backs up something I saw with my own eyes: children can hold more than one secure relationship at a time. That's part of why a new teacher who's genuinely warm and consistent can become a real source of comfort, not just a stand-in for the one who left.

None of this means children don't feel the change. It means they can experience it differently  through "who's here with me right now" rather than "who used to be here." And how any one child responds still depends on their personality, their history, their age, and this part matters a lot how well the transition itself is handled. Some kids sail through it. Others genuinely struggle, and that struggle deserves to be taken seriously, not brushed off with "kids are so resilient."

If I'm honest, I think adults sometimes feel our own discomfort with change first, and assume the child feels it too, before we've actually watched how they respond.

## Is changing teachers every year actually good for kids?

I don't think there's a clean yes or no here.

There's a real case for some change. Meeting different adults can build a child's confidence and flexibility. Different teachers bring different strengths and different kinds of warmth, and that variety can genuinely add something. It can also stop a child from getting so attached to one adult that they can't function with anyone else.

But there's just as real a case against too much turnover. When turnover is high, children tend to show weaker gains both academically and emotionally and teacher consistency plays a real role in how secure kids feel. One study of Head Start children found that those who went through more teacher turnover during the year had smaller gains in vocabulary and literacy, and more behavior concerns reported by parents. And it's not just the child — families themselves often find it harder to build trust with a new teacher and get more anxious about their child's emotional security when the staff keeps changing.

Here's the distinction I've landed on: there's a real difference between *healthy exposure to different educators as part of a well-designed program* and *frequent, unpredictable turnover because people keep leaving*. The first is a choice, planned and supported. The second is just disruption — however gracefully a school handles it. The mistake is treating the second like it's the first.

## What the research actually says about continuity

The research here is older than you'd think, and fairly consistent though most of it focuses on infants and toddlers rather than preschoolers specifically. One of the classic studies, by Helen Raikes in 1993, found that babies who stayed with the same caregiver for over a year were far more likely to develop a secure attachment than babies whose caregiver changed within nine to twelve months, or within five to eight months. Time with one consistent adult clearly matters for how safe a young child feels.

More recent research on "looping" where a child stays with the same teacher for two or more years instead of switching every year points the same direction. One case study found that children who spent two years with the same teacher were more likely to form a strong bond with them, and so were their parents. Broader looping research has also found small gains in academic performance and fewer behavior issues.

But here's what the research doesn't say: it doesn't hand you a magic number of months. What it says, again and again, is something less tidy stability and warmth matter more than any specific timeline. A child with an average teacher for eighteen months isn't automatically better off than a child with a wonderful one for eight.

## Is there an ideal length of time?

I went looking for a number. Twelve months, eighteen months, two years — something I could point to and say, "this is the standard." I didn't find one.

Some infant-toddler programs keep a child with the same teacher for their first three years specifically because of the security it builds, and some early-childhood guidance describes continuity of up to three years as an ideal while admitting most programs find that hard to actually pull off. So "ideal" here reads more like a direction to aim for than a rule with a hard cutoff.

Which is why I don't think the useful question is *how many months should one teacher stay?* I think the better question the one I actually ask myself now is *how stable and safe is this child's whole environment, no matter who's standing in front of the classroom this year?* A teacher change inside a calm, well-run school feels completely different to a child than the same change inside a chaotic one.

## The educator's side of this

It wouldn't be fair to make this about educators being the problem. People leave for reasons that have nothing to do with the children they cared for a move, a family need, a better opportunity, or just burnout after the kind of intensity this job asks of you. A school can't always prevent that, and shouldn't pretend it can.

Which means the job of a good school isn't to promise that one particular teacher stays forever. It's to build something sturdy enough that a child stays supported even when the person in front of them changes where what a child needs is written down and shared, not locked away in one teacher's memory.

## What I learned as a school leader

The operational side of a sudden resignation is, honestly, the easy part. You find someone, brief them, adjust the schedule. The harder part is everything around it steadying the rest of your staff, supporting the children through the handover, and talking to parents in a way that's honest without scaring them.

I learned that parents need reassurance almost as much as children need continuity, and that these two things aren't actually in competition. A calm parent passes calm on to their child far more effectively than any activity we plan in the classroom.

I learned that trust in a preschool often lives in the one face a parent sees every morning at drop-off  which is exactly why that trust feels so shaky when the face changes, and why explaining the "why" behind a departure, as much as I honestly can, matters more than I used to think.

And I learned, in a slightly humbling way, that the children were teaching me something about change that I hadn't fully understood myself: that feeling safe doesn't require everything to stay the same. It can come from warmth and consistency of care, delivered by more than one person over time.

I don't want to make this sound tidier than it was. Some children did struggle clung a little longer, tested the new teacher a bit harder, needed more one-on-one time before they settled. That response was just as valid as the child who adjusted in a day. What actually made the difference, in both cases, wasn't the new teacher's personality alone. It was how carefully we handled the transition itself — introducing her properly before the old teacher left, keeping the daily routine unchanged, passing on what we knew about each child's habits, and giving both kids and parents the space to ask questions without feeling judged for it.


I don't think a good preschool's job is to make sure a child never goes through change. No school can honestly promise that. I think the real job is smaller and more doable than that: to make sure that when change does happen, the child still feels safe enough to keep playing, keep learning, and keep trusting the people around them.

Maybe that's the actual work of running a school — not keeping everything the same, but building something sturdy enough to absorb the change when it comes.
